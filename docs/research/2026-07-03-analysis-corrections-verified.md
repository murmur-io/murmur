And the market *can* do on-device voiceprinting (Apple already does, and got sued for it).
- **Real 2026 cases the user cited, both verified:** **Zaluda et al. v. Wash., No. 2:26-cv-00422, filed Feb 5 2026) — targets Teams live-transcription **diarization** (speaker attribution); "if the court says yes, every SaaS product that attributes speech to individual speakers has a BIPA problem."

CIPA (all-party recording consent) is a **separate, always-on** baseline, orthogonal to voiceprint.

## 3. AGPL — my morning brief had it BACKWARDS · verdict: user CORRECT (two real qualifications)
- **AGPL is copyleft, not a moat-leak.** A competitor *can* fork, but any distributed/network-served derivative **must itself be AGPL** (source-available, network-copyleft) — they **cannot** ship a proprietary product from it. The **only** party who can grant a proprietary (copyleft-free) exception is the **copyright owner** (FSF "selling exceptions"). So **AGPL + consolidated copyright (via a CLA) = the MySQL / Grafana / GitLab dual-licensing MOAT** — durability *rises* with a CLA, it doesn't fall. My "AGPL lets a funded competitor fork the moat [proprietarily]" was wrong.

- **Qualification B (minor):** the enterprise AGPL ban is *primarily* about network-service/SaaS incorporation (the regulated/self-hosted "Fork B" path), so a single-user desktop install is *essentially* never affected — but Google's *written* policy also bans installing AGPL software on employee laptops as belt-and-suspenders, so a locked-down MDM fleet **could** still block even a desktop install. "Only hits Fork B" is directionally right, slightly overstated as absolute.
